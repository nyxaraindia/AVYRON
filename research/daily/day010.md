\## 1. Research Objective



Investigate how AVYRON can organize its software into reusable, independently testable modules.



Today's research focused on:



\- Separating software responsibilities.

\- Reusing common functionality.

\- Supporting heterogeneous robot hardware.

\- Designing module communication.

\- Preserving independent safety authority.

\- Preventing intelligence from executing rejected actions.



\## 2. Proposed Module Organization



A preliminary module structure was identified:



```text

AVYRON/

│

├── main.py

├── battery.py

├── camera.py

├── control.py

├── capabilities.py

└── safety.py

```



\### Responsibilities



| Module | Responsibility |

|---|---|

| main.py | System coordination |

| battery.py | Battery monitoring |

| camera.py | Camera information |

| control.py | Robot-specific control |

| capabilities.py | Capability assessment |

| safety.py | Independent safety authorization |



This is an early conceptual organization, not a finalized implementation.



\## 3. Research Finding: Separation of Responsibilities



The learner identified that battery monitoring and movement control should be separate because they perform different tasks.



This separation can improve:



\- Testing.

\- Debugging.

\- Maintenance.

\- Reusability.

\- Development organization.



A battery module should not claim that an entire movement is safe merely because the battery has sufficient charge.



\### Principle



\*\*A module must not claim authority beyond its responsibility.\*\*



\## 4. Research Finding: Common Interfaces and Different Implementations



Different robot embodiments can have different hardware systems.



Example:



| Robot | Battery system |

|---|---|

| Humanoid | Battery pack A |

| Drone | Battery pack B |

| Wheeled robot | Battery pack C |



A common interface can allow AVYRON's higher-level components to consume normalized battery information.



Hardware-specific adapters can handle differences between the physical systems.



This is a candidate architectural direction requiring implementation and validation.



\## 5. Independent Safety Authority



A central research question was whether intelligence should be able to bypass safety rejection.



The conclusion was:



\*\*No. Independent safety authority must not be bypassed by the intelligence module.\*\*



\### Example



Robot condition:



\- Battery: 80%.

\- Gripper: Working.

\- Movement capability: Available.

\- Safety assessment: Rejected.



The system must not execute the rejected movement.



Battery readiness, capability availability and safety authorization are different concepts.



A robot can have the physical capability to perform an action while still being unsafe to perform it under its current conditions.



\## 6. Safety Authorization vs Motor Control



The learner proposed that the safety module should authorize or reject actions instead of directly controlling robot motors.



A conceptual separation:



\- Intelligence prepares an action proposal.

\- Safety independently evaluates and authorizes or rejects it.

\- The control mechanism executes only permitted commands within their authorization limits.



The actual runtime enforcement mechanism remains an implementation and research task.



A safety rejection must not be bypassable through another software path.



\## 7. Module Communication Research



A preliminary communication sequence was proposed:



1\. Import or access battery information.

2\. Access capability information.

3\. Obtain perception information.

4\. Request safety authorization.

5\. Allow movement control only when authorization is valid.



\### Improved conceptual workflow



```text

Perception

&#x20;   |

&#x20;   v

Battery and Capability Assessment

&#x20;   |

&#x20;   v

Intelligence / Planning

&#x20;   |

&#x20;   v

Action Proposal

&#x20;   |

&#x20;   v

Independent Safety Authorization

&#x20;   |

&#x20;   +---- Rejected ----> Block Action

&#x20;   |                    Report and Reassess

&#x20;   |

&#x20;   +---- Authorized --> Control Mechanism

&#x20;                        |

&#x20;                        v

&#x20;                     Execution

&#x20;                        |

&#x20;                        v

&#x20;                     Feedback

```



This is a conceptual workflow. The modules do not necessarily need to communicate in a single rigid sequence; an implementation may use events, services, data streams or other communication mechanisms.



\## 8. Research Challenge



\### Scenario



A humanoid robot receives a task:



> Pick up a 5 kg box and move it to another location.



Module reports:



\- Battery: 90%, ready.

\- Perception: Box detected.

\- Capability: Gripper and lifting capability available.

\- Movement: Strategy prepared.

\- Safety: Action rejected because the robot's current position is unstable.



\### Learner's answers and evaluation



\*\*Question 1: Should intelligence execute the action after safety rejection?\*\*



Initial answer: The learner first indicated that intelligence might execute the rejected action.



Correction: This is not permitted. A rejected action must remain blocked.



\*\*Question 2: Should safety directly control motors or authorize actions?\*\*



Learner's answer: Safety should authorize the action, not directly control the motors.



Evaluation: Correct architectural separation.



\*\*Question 3: If the robot becomes stable later, should the old action execute automatically?\*\*



Learner's answer: Recheck the situation.



Evaluation: Correct. The task and action must be reassessed against current conditions before a new authorization.



\*\*Question 4: Design the communication sequence.\*\*



Learner's proposed sequence:



Battery → Capability → Perception → Safety → Movement.



Evaluation: The main modules were identified. The sequence was refined to include intelligence/planning before safety authorization and to make the authorization result control whether execution is permitted.



\### Research challenge result



\*\*3/4.\*\*



The first answer required correction; the remaining answers demonstrated an understanding of modular responsibility and independent safety.



\## 9. Important Architectural Refinements



\### 9.1 Rejected actions



A safety-rejected action must not be executed by intelligence or another module.



\### 9.2 Reassessment



If robot conditions change, the system must reassess the action rather than reuse an old decision blindly.



\### 9.3 Authorization validity



An authorization should be associated with the specific action, robot state, constraints and validity conditions it covers.



Relevant state changes may invalidate authorization.



\### 9.4 Task isolation



A rejected movement should block the affected action. Unrelated tasks may continue only if their own safety conditions remain satisfied.



\### 9.5 Independent enforcement



Safety authority must be enforced by the runtime/control architecture, not merely represented by a software field or a promise that intelligence will obey.



\## 10. Research Principles Identified



1\. Common intelligence should be separated from robot-specific implementations.

2\. Modules should have clearly defined responsibilities.

3\. Reusable functionality should be shared through interfaces.

4\. Hardware-specific differences should be handled by appropriate adapters.

5\. Battery readiness is not equivalent to task readiness.

6\. Capability availability is not equivalent to safety authorization.

7\. Intelligence cannot override independent safety rejection.

8\. Changed conditions require reassessment.

9\. Safety enforcement must not depend solely on voluntary cooperation from the intelligence module.



\## 11. Open Research Questions



Further research is required into:

\- How can safety authorization be enforced independently?

\- How should authorization expire after relevant state changes?

\- How can different robot hardware interfaces be normalized?

\- How should the system handle communication failure between intelligence and safety?

\- How can module-level testing support whole-system verification?

\## 13. Research Conclusion



Day 10 established modular architecture as an important direction for AVYRON.



The project should support reusable common components while allowing different robot embodiments to use hardware-specific implementations.



The most important finding was the distinction between intelligence authority and safety authority.



Intelligence can propose and plan actions, but independent safety mechanisms must retain the ability to reject them. Rejected actions must remain blocked, and changed conditions require reassessment.



These are architectural principles to implement and validate, not yet experimentally proven properties of AVYRON.
